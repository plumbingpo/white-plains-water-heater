How Harrison Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Most early failures trace back to skipped maintenance, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Drain and flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and operate the relief valve each year so it doesn't seize. Keep the temperature near 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. For a tankless heater in a hard-water area, plan on descaling once a year so the heat exchanger stays clean.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually includes the month and year it was built, and its age tells you a lot. Most tank heaters reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is beyond that point and is also showing discolored water, popping sounds, or moisture at the base, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than an emergency replacement once the tank fails.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or a missing expansion tank, both modest fixes. The same drip ignored for months can keep stressing the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

The majority of water heater failures we see in Harrison come back to what's in the water. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Age matters just as much. Many homes around here still have the original tank, and beyond the ten-year mark, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust begins attacking the steel from the inside. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Every house is set up a little differently. A home on a private well may see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, often fixed by switching the anode rod type. A tankless heater in hard water needs annual descaling. And an older installation being replaced often needs updates that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a properly routed relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less to address early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
